For \(L\ne 0\), let \(\alpha \) and \(\beta \) be the decreasing lists of positive eigenvalues of \(S\) and absolute values of its negative eigenvalues, padded with zeros to equal length. Define \(\pi (L)=(a,b,c,d)\) by

\[ a=\frac{\lVert T\rVert ^2}{\lVert L\rVert ^2},\qquad b=\frac{2\langle \alpha ,\beta \rangle }{\lVert L\rVert ^2},\qquad c=\frac{\lVert (\alpha -\beta )_+\rVert ^2}{\lVert L\rVert ^2},\qquad d=\frac{\lVert (\alpha -\beta )_-\rVert ^2}{\lVert L\rVert ^2}. \]

Here \(x_+=\max (x,0)\) and \(x_-=\max (-x,0)\), taken entrywise. Lean pads both lists to length \(N\) and includes the denominator in each definition.

If the symmetric part \(S\) of \(L\) is nonzero, \(\pi (L)=(a,b,c,d)\) and \(\pi (S)=(0,b_S,c_S,d_S)\), then \((b,c,d)=(1-a)(b_S,c_S,d_S)\).

Proof ▼

The two profiles are computed from the same eigenvalues and differ only in the normalization. The lists \(\alpha \) and \(\beta \) of \(L\) and of \(S\) are the same, since \(S\) is its own symmetric part. So \(b,c,d\) and \(b_S,c_S,d_S\) are the same expressions in \(\alpha ,\beta \), divided by \(\lVert L\rVert ^2\) and by \(\lVert S\rVert ^2\) respectively. By Lemma 5, \(\lVert S\rVert ^2/\lVert L\rVert ^2=1-\lVert T\rVert ^2/\lVert L\rVert ^2=1-a\).

If \(L\) has rank one and unit norm, then with \(t=\operatorname {tr}(L)\),

\[ \pi (L)=\Bigl(\frac{1-t^2}2,\frac{1-t^2}2,(t_+)^2,(t_-)^2\Bigr). \]

For \(N\ge 2\) the set of profiles of rank-one matrices is \(\Theta =\{ a=b,\ cd=0\} \subset \Delta _3\). (For \(N=1\) only the two vertices \(c=1\) and \(d=1\) occur, so the set statement needs \(N\ge 2\).)

Proof ▼

After an orthogonal change of coordinates, which does not change the profile, \(L\) is zero outside a \(2\times 2\) block whose symmetric and antisymmetric parts can be read off. Write \(L=uv^T\) with unit vectors \(u,v\). The only possible nonzero eigenvalue of \(L\) is \(\langle u,v\rangle \), with eigenvector \(u\), so \(t=\langle u,v\rangle \). If \(u=\pm v\), then in a basis starting with \(u\) the matrix \(L\) has a single nonzero entry \(\pm 1\), and its profile is \((0,0,1,0)\) or \((0,0,0,1)\). If \(|t|{\lt}1\), the vectors \(e=(u+v)/\sqrt{2+2t}\) and \(f=(v-u)/\sqrt{2-2t}\) are orthonormal. In an orthonormal basis that starts with \(e,f\), the matrix \(L\) is zero except for the top-left block \(\tfrac 12\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1+t & \sqrt{1-t^2} \\ -\sqrt{1-t^2} & t-1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). Its symmetric part is \(\operatorname {diag}((1+t)/2,(t-1)/2)\), and its antisymmetric part has \(\lVert T\rVert ^2=(1-t^2)/2\). So \(\alpha =((1+t)/2)\) and \(\beta =((1-t)/2)\), padded with zeros, which give \(b=2\alpha _1\beta _1=(1-t^2)/2\), \(c=(t_+)^2\) and \(d=(t_-)^2\).

The paper does not spell out the set equality. The Lean proof applies the same computation to \(s\, uv^T\) with \(s{\gt}0\), which covers every rank-one matrix, and obtains every point of \(\Theta \) from \(u=e_1\) and \(v=te_1+\sqrt{1-t^2}\, e_2\) with \(t\in [-1,1]\); this uses \(N\ge 2\).

Let \(X{\gt}0\) have finite nonzero second moment, fix \(u,v{\gt}0\) and \(\rho =u/v\). Let \(N_n\ge 2n\) and let \(S_n\in M_{N_n}(\mathbb {R})\) be symmetric with eigenvalues \(vX_1,\ldots ,vX_n\), \(-uX'_1,\ldots ,-uX'_n\) and zeros, where the \(X_i,X'_i\) are independent copies of \(X\). Then almost surely

\[ \pi (S_n)\longrightarrow \frac1{1+\rho ^2}\bigl(0,\, 2\rho ,\, (1-\rho )_+^2,\, (\rho -1)_+^2\bigr)\in p(\Theta ), \]

where \(p(a,b,c,d)=(0,a+b,c,d)\). As \(\rho \) ranges over \((0,1]\) the limit traces the edge \(\{ d=0\} \), and over \([1,\infty )\) the edge \(\{ c=0\} \).

Proof ▼

By Lemma 6 the two sorted lists of eigenvalues become proportional, and each coordinate of the profile, after dividing numerator and denominator by \(\lVert \alpha _n\rVert ^2\), is a ratio of quantities that converge. Let \(\alpha _n\) and \(\beta _n\) be the positive eigenvalues of \(S_n\) and the absolute values of its negative eigenvalues, each in decreasing order. By Lemma 6, \(\beta _n=\rho \alpha _n+\varepsilon _n\) with \(\delta _n=\lVert \varepsilon _n\rVert /\lVert \alpha _n\rVert \to 0\) almost surely. The Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities give \(|\langle \alpha _n,\beta _n\rangle /\lVert \alpha _n\rVert ^2-\rho |\le \delta _n\) and \(|\lVert \beta _n\rVert ^2/\lVert \alpha _n\rVert ^2-\rho ^2|\le \delta _n^2+2\rho \delta _n\). Since \(x\mapsto x_\pm \) is \(1\)-Lipschitz in each entry and \(((1-\rho )\alpha _n)_\pm =(1-\rho )_\pm \alpha _n\), also \(|\lVert (\alpha _n-\beta _n)_\pm \rVert /\lVert \alpha _n\rVert -(1-\rho )_\pm |\le \delta _n\). Dividing the numerators and the denominator \(\lVert S_n\rVert ^2=\lVert \alpha _n\rVert ^2+\lVert \beta _n\rVert ^2\) by \(\lVert \alpha _n\rVert ^2\), the ratios converge to the stated limit.

The paper does not prove the remaining claims; the Lean proof does so as follows. The limit lies in \(p(\Theta )\) because its coordinates are nonnegative, sum to one, and its last two have product zero. For the edges, \(b(\rho )=2\rho /(1+\rho ^2)\) is a strictly increasing bijection from \((0,1]\) onto \((0,1]\) and a strictly decreasing bijection from \([1,\infty )\) onto \((0,1]\).
